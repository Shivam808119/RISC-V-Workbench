01

What "faster" means

Time is the only honest yardstick. Everything else is a stand-in for it.

Part 1 · What "faster" means

Performance is the inverse of time

Definitions

performance = 1 ÷ execution time
"X is n times faster than Y" ⇔ n = time(Y) ÷ time(X)

  • Example: a program takes 15 s on machine Y and 10 s on X. X is 15 ÷ 10 = 1.5× faster.
  • Two different questions need two different measures: how long does my one job take (latency)? How many jobs does the machine finish per hour (throughput)? A laptop user cares about the first; a web server about the second.
  • Every number in this deck is either a time, or a rate derived from one.
Part 1 · What "faster" means

Rate metrics, and why they mislead

MIPS

Millions of instructions per second = instruction count ÷ (time × 10⁶). Easy to quote — but an instruction on one ISA does different work from one on another, and even on one machine, a program of many simple instructions scores higher while finishing later.

FLOPS

Floating-point operations per second (MFLOPS, GFLOPS, TFLOPS). Meaningful for number-crunching — when everyone counts operations the same way, on the same algorithm.

Worked example: higher MIPS, slower machine

Machine A runs a job as 2.0 billion simple instructions in 1.0 s: 2 000 MIPS. Machine B runs the same job as 1.2 billion richer instructions in 0.8 s: 1 500 MIPS.

B has the lower MIPS — and finishes 1.25× sooner. MIPS rewarded A for doing more, smaller steps.

02

Synthetic benchmarks: Dhrystone

A small made-up program, run in a loop, standing in for "typical" code.

Part 2 · Synthetic benchmarks: Dhrystone

Synthetic benchmarks

  • A synthetic benchmark is a small artificial program written to imitate the mix of operations in typical software — not a real application.
  • Small and portable: easy to compile for any processor, even one with no operating system.
  • But "typical" is a guess, and a small program behaves unlike a large one — as we'll see.

Whetstone1972floating-point arithmetic and maths functionsMWIPS

Part 2 · Synthetic benchmarks: Dhrystone

Dhrystone and DMIPS

  • Dhrystone, by Reinhold Weicker, runs one loop over and over; the raw result is Dhrystones per second (loops per second).
  • The VAX-11/780 — marketed as a "1 MIPS" machine — scored 1 757 Dhrystones per second. So the score is divided by 1 757 and called DMIPS: "how many VAXes is this?"
  • Divide by the clock in MHz and you get DMIPS/MHz — work per clock tick, a CPI-like figure that compares designs at different clock rates.

Worked example

351 400 Dhrystones/s ÷ 1 757 = 200 DMIPS

200 DMIPS ÷ 100 MHz = 2.0 DMIPS/MHz

Part 2 · Synthetic benchmarks: Dhrystone

Why Dhrystone is retiring

  • Tiny: code and data fit in any cache, so it never feels the memory wall.
  • Compiler-sensitive: clever compilers can remove or simplify parts of it. Much of the score comes from string copying, which some libraries hand-optimise for exactly this test.
  • No enforced rules: vendors chose their own compiler, flags and libraries. Two "DMIPS" figures are rarely comparable.
Rule

Treat a DMIPS number as a rough hint only — and only when the compiler and flags are stated.

03

CoreMark and Embench

Modern, open benchmarks for small and embedded processors.

Part 3 · CoreMark and Embench

CoreMark: Dhrystone, done properly

CoreMark (EEMBC, 2009) is a small, free benchmark for processor cores, designed to fix Dhrystone's flaws.

Lists

find and sort in linked lists — pointer chasing

Matrices

multiply and add — loops and arithmetic

State machine

parse input — branchy, hard to predict

CRC

checks every result — so nothing can be optimised away

  • Results are computed from data the compiler can't see in advance, and self-checked.
  • Run rules: run for at least 10 seconds, don't change the source, and report the compiler and flags with every score.
  • Score = iterations per second; CoreMark/MHz divides by the clock.

Part 3 · CoreMark and Embench

Reading CoreMark/MHz

Kind of coreRough CoreMark/MHzWhy
tiny multicycle or bit-serialwell below 1many cycles per instruction
simple in-order microcontrollerabout 2–4pipelined, about one instruction per cycle
superscalar in-orderabout 3–5sometimes two per cycle

Rough orders of magnitude for one core, from published results; exact figures depend on compiler and flags — which is the point of reporting them.

Per MHz is not the whole story

A design with high CoreMark/MHz may only reach a low clock rate. The chip's real speed is CoreMark/MHz × MHz — the Iron Law again.

Part 3 · CoreMark and Embench

More embedded benchmarks

Embench

A free, open suite (2019) of about 20 real embedded programs. Reports speed relative to a reference core and code size, both as geometric means (the n-th root of the product of the ratios — explained below) — because in embedded, memory costs money.

CoreMark-PRO

EEMBC's bigger sibling: several integer and floating-point workloads with larger data, closer to real applications.

ULPMark

EEMBC's energy benchmark for microcontrollers: how much energy a standard task costs.

04

Big-system benchmarks

Real applications, whole systems, and specialised workloads.

Part 4 · Big-system benchmarks

SPEC CPU 2017

  • From the Standard Performance Evaluation Corporation: 43 benchmarks built from real applications — compilers, compression, physics, weather, chess…
  • Four suites: integer and floating point, each measured two ways:

SPECspeed

One copy of each program: how quickly does one job finish? (latency)

SPECrate

Many copies at once: how much work per hour? (throughput — fills every core)

  • Each result is a ratio against a fixed reference machine's time; the overall score is the geometric mean of the ratios.
  • Base results use the same conservative flags for all programs; peak allows per-program tuning.
  • Strict run and reporting rules, and published, reviewed results.

05

Summarising and comparing

Many numbers into one — without fooling yourself.

Part 5 · Summarising and comparing

Which mean?

  • For raw times of a fixed workload, the total (or arithmetic mean) of times is honest: it is how long the work takes.
  • For results normalised to a reference machine, use the geometric mean: the n-th root of the product of n ratios. It gives the same ranking whichever machine is the reference.

Part 5 · Summarising and comparing

Faster isn't everything: efficiency

Performance per watt

CoreMark/mW, SPECrate per watt. Decides battery life and data-centre electricity bills.

Energy per operation

Picojoules per instruction. Energy = power × time, so a fast chip that finishes and sleeps can use less energy than a slow, frugal one.

Performance per area

CoreMark/mm². Silicon costs money; this is how many cores fit on a die.

Worked example

Core X: 4.0 CoreMark/MHz at 1 000 MHz, 0.5 W → 4 000 CoreMark, 8 000 CoreMark/W.
Core Y: 6.0 CoreMark/MHz at 2 000 MHz, 3 W → 12 000 CoreMark, 4 000 CoreMark/W.

Y is 3× faster; X does twice the work per joule. Which is "better" depends on the product — a server or a watch.

Benchmarking honestly

How results get bent, and how to report them straight.

Part 6 · Benchmarking honestly

Tricks and traps

TrapWhat happens
Benchmark-specific tuningcompilers or libraries recognise a famous benchmark and special-case it
Hidden flagsthe headline uses aggressive flags; your code won't
Cherry-pickingquote only the tests you win
Fits in cachea tiny benchmark never touches main memory, so it hides the memory wall
Unequal systemsmore memory, a faster disk or a higher power limit on one side
Peak vs sustaineda burst at turbo clock, before the chip heats up and slows down
Part 6 · Benchmarking honestly

A reporting checklist

  • State the exact workload and input size.
  • State the system: core, clock (fixed or turbo), memory, cache sizes.
  • State the software: compiler and version, every flag, libraries.
  • Repeat runs; report the median and the spread; let caches and clocks warm up first.
  • Use the right mean, and show the per-program results too.
  • Make it reproducible: someone else should get the same number.
The golden rule

The best benchmark is the program you actually care about. Every other score is an estimate of it.

Part 6 · Benchmarking honestly

Check yourself

1. A core at 250 MHz scores 1 230 000 Dhrystones/s. What are its DMIPS and DMIPS/MHz?
2. A CoreMark run completes 30 000 iterations in 15 s on a 500 MHz core. Score and CoreMark/MHz?
3. Why is a geometric mean used for SPEC's ratios?

Answer

1. 1 230 000 ÷ 1 757 ≈ 700 DMIPS; ÷ 250 = 2.8 DMIPS/MHz.

2. 30 000 ÷ 15 = 2 000; ÷ 500 = 4.0 CoreMark/MHz.

3. The results are ratios against a reference; the geometric mean gives the same ranking whichever reference is chosen, where an arithmetic mean of ratios can flip.

Part 6 · Benchmarking honestly

Recap

  • Performance = 1 ÷ time. Rate metrics such as MIPS mislead across ISAs and programs; FLOPS only for like-for-like number crunching.
  • Dhrystone → DMIPS (÷ 1 757) and DMIPS/MHz: historic, tiny, easily gamed. CoreMark fixes it with self-checking, run rules and CoreMark/MHz; Embench adds open, real programs and code size.
  • SPEC CPU 2017 uses real applications, speed and rate, ratios against a reference; LINPACK, STREAM and MLPerf each measure one thing.
  • Geometric mean for normalised results; compare efficiency per watt, per joule and per mm² as well as speed.
  • Report workload, system, compiler and flags; repeat runs. The best benchmark is your own program.
